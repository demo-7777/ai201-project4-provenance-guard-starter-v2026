# Acceptance criteria — Provenance Guard

Five criteria that say what "working" means for this service, written in unit 7
**before** you built it.

**All five are yours.** None are given.

An acceptance criterion names a number or something plainly observable. *"The
detector is accurate"* is an opinion — and perfect AI detection isn't possible
anyway, so it's an opinion about something nobody can deliver. *"Of 10 pieces I
wrote myself, at most 1 is labelled high-confidence AI"* is a criterion.

Under each, write a sentence or two on **why that target**. *"I allow at most 1
false positive in 10 because on a writing site a wrong accusation costs more
than a missed detection"* is a real answer.

> Missing your own targets next unit costs you nothing. Setting a target so
> easy you can't miss it does.

---

## Pick numbers you can defend

**Cover at least three of these five areas.** They're prompts, not a form.

| Area | A question it could answer |
|---|---|
| False positives | How often may a human's writing get called AI? |
| Score spread | How far apart should clearly-AI and clearly-human text score? |
| Label coverage | Is every one of your three labels actually reachable? |
| The appeal path | Does an appeal always change status and always get recorded? |
| Bad input | What happens when `text` is missing, empty, or enormous? |

Two things worth knowing before you pick numbers:

- **The expensive mistake is one-sided.** Missing some AI text costs the site a
  little. Accusing a real writer costs that person a lot. A criterion that
  treats both errors as equally bad is describing a system nobody would ship.
- **Unit 8 tests across three trials, and the target has to hold across all
  three.** Your first signal runs a model, so identical input can score
  differently twice. A target of "at most 1 in 10" against trials of 2, 1, 2 is
  a **miss**.

---
## 1. Bad Input Handling

<!-- Your criterion. It must name a number. -->
Given these 5 bad inputs sent to POST /submit — `text` missing, `text` empty (`""`), `text` whitespace-only, `text` too large (>9999 characters), and `text` that is not a string (e.g. a number) — the system returns a 400 response with an `error` field for all 5 of 5, and never returns a 500 or a label.


**Why this target:** It is impossible for the system to return a correct label on a `text` that is too large, empty, whitespace-only, missing, or not a string. The system should also be guaranteed to check for each of these before any signal runs.



---

## 2. The Appeal Path

<!-- Your criterion. -->
Given 5 appeals sent to POST /appeal, each with a real content_id from an earlier submission and a non-empty reasoning, all 5 of 5 return status: "under_review" in the response, and GET /log shows a new appeal entry for each with that content_id, status: "under_review", and the reasoning text. Given 1 appeal with a content_id that was never submitted, the system returns a 404 and adds no appeal entry to the log.


**Why this target:** This criterion is to ensure the deterministic part of the appeal path is working. Sending the appeal should be a consistent procedure that sets up the work for later review by a human.



---

## 3. False Positives

<!-- Your criterion. -->
Given 10 human-written pieces published before 2020 (before AI writing tools were widely available) — such as blog posts, forum answers, or essays, 150–300 words each, with at least 5 written in a plain, simple style — each submitted to POST /submit, at most 1 returns `guess: "ai"` (the high-confidence AI label). An `unsure` result does not count as a false positive.


**Why this target:** The cost for a false positive is significant to the writer. The system can't guarantee 100% false positive prevention, therefore significantly low rate of false positives may be acceptable.



---

## 4. Label Coverage

<!-- Your criterion. -->
Given a set of 9 submissions — 3 clearly human pieces published before 2020, 3 generated by an AI model, and 3 I judge borderline (lightly-edited AI or plain human writing) — submitted to POST /submit, the system assigns each of the three labels (`human`, `unsure`, `ai`) to at least one submission.


**Why this target:** This target is to ensure that for each label the system can assign, there exists at least one combined score the system can arrive at such that that score corresponds to that label. With thresholds at 0.35 and 0.65, a combining rule that averages the two signals can get stuck in the middle — for example, while the style signal is a stub returning 0.5, the combined score can never leave 0.25–0.75 — so a label being unreachable is a real risk, not a formality.



---

## 5. Score Spread

<!-- Your criterion. -->
Given 5 submissions that are clearly AI (generated by an AI model, unedited) and 5 submissions that are clearly human (published before 2020), each submitted to POST /submit, the average combined score (`confidence`) of the clearly AI submissions is at least 0.5 greater than the average combined score of the clearly human submissions.


**Why this target:** This target is to ensure that the score assigned to a submission is meaningful. Two submissions labeled AI and human but differed by 0.1 points for example would have very little significance. A gap of 0.5 is wider than my unsure band (0.35–0.65, 0.30 wide), so typical AI and typical human pieces should land in different labels rather than both falling into "unsure".
